# 未見文 第 36 群（第 531 組）: 入試長文に近い自作の段落 2 本（消えゆく言語の説明文 15 文・動物保護施設でのボランティアの物語 15 文）を 1 文ずつ
Of the roughly seven thousand languages spoken in the world today, nearly half are in danger of disappearing by the end of this century.
A language usually dies not because people suddenly stop speaking it, but because parents gradually stop passing it on to their children.
When a community moves to a city or comes into contact with a more powerful culture, young people often find that speaking the dominant language brings better jobs and higher status.
Over time, the older language comes to be seen as old-fashioned, and children may even feel embarrassed to use it in public.
Once the last fluent speakers pass away, much of the knowledge stored in the language is lost with them.
This matters because every language reflects a unique way of understanding the world.
Some languages, for example, have dozens of words for different kinds of snow or rain, while others describe directions in terms of mountains and rivers rather than left and right.
Linguists also point out that many traditional names for plants contain information about how they can be used as medicine.
Fortunately, a number of communities are working hard to bring their languages back to life.
In New Zealand, preschools where children hear only the Maori language have helped thousands of young people become fluent.
Other groups are recording elderly speakers and creating online dictionaries so that future generations can learn from them.
Technology, which once seemed to threaten small languages, may now turn out to be one of their most powerful tools.
However, experts warn that recordings alone cannot keep a language alive.
What really matters is whether people continue to use it in their daily lives, at home and with their friends.
After all, a language survives only as long as there are people who want to speak it.
Last spring, I started volunteering at an animal shelter near my house every Saturday morning.
I had always wanted a dog, but my apartment building did not allow pets, so this seemed like the next best thing.
On my first day, a staff member named Mrs. Brown showed me how to clean the cages and prepare food for the animals.
The work was harder than I had expected, and by noon my arms and back were sore.
Still, I enjoyed spending time with the dogs, especially an old brown one called Max who never barked at anyone.
Mrs. Brown told me that Max had been at the shelter for almost two years because most visitors preferred younger dogs.
Every week, I took him for a walk around the park and talked to him as if he could understand every word.
One Saturday in July, I arrived at the shelter and found that his cage was empty.
For a moment, I was afraid that something terrible had happened to him.
Then Mrs. Brown smiled and handed me a photo of Max sitting happily between an elderly couple on a sofa.
They had adopted him the day before, after their own dog had died the previous winter.
I felt a little sad that I would not be able to see him again, but I was much happier for him.
Since then, I have helped many other animals find new homes, and each time I feel the same mix of sadness and joy.
Volunteering has taught me that small acts of kindness can change the lives of others, even if no one notices them.
I am still not allowed to keep a dog, but I no longer mind as much as I used to.
