# 未見文 第 40 群（第 535 組）: 入試長文に近い自作の段落 2 本（忘却曲線と分散学習の説明文 15 文・島の祖母を一人で訪ねた旅の物語 15 文）を 1 文ずつ
Most of us have had the experience of studying hard for a test, only to forget almost everything a few weeks later.
More than a hundred years ago, a German researcher named Hermann Ebbinghaus decided to study this problem scientifically.
He memorized long lists of meaningless syllables and then tested himself at different intervals to see how much he still remembered.
His results showed that memory fades very quickly at first and then more slowly over time.
Within a single day, he had forgotten more than half of what he had learned.
This pattern, now known as the forgetting curve, has been confirmed by many later studies.
Fortunately, Ebbinghaus also discovered a way to slow down forgetting.
Each time he reviewed the material, it took him less time to relearn it, and he remembered it for longer afterward.
Today, this technique is called spaced repetition, and it is one of the most effective ways to learn.
Instead of studying something many times in one night, learners review it several times over a period of days or weeks.
The gaps between reviews allow the brain to strengthen the memory each time it is recalled.
Many language-learning apps now use this idea to decide when to show users words they are likely to forget.
Of course, forgetting is not always a bad thing.
If we remembered every detail of every day, it would be much harder to focus on what really matters.
In a sense, forgetting helps us make room for new and more useful information.
Every summer, my family used to visit my grandmother, who lives alone on a small island in the south of Japan.
To get there, we had to take a train, a bus, and finally a ferry that ran only twice a day.
Last year, however, my parents were too busy to go, so I made the trip by myself for the first time.
I was a little worried, but my mother wrote down every step for me on a piece of paper.
The train and the bus were easy, and I arrived at the port about an hour before the ferry was supposed to leave.
While I was waiting, the sky suddenly grew dark and a strong wind began to blow.
A man at the ticket office told me that the ferry had been canceled because of the storm.
I did not know what to do, and for a moment I felt like crying.
Then an old fisherman who had overheard our conversation came over and asked where I was going.
When I told him my grandmother's name, he smiled and said that he knew her very well.
He let me wait in his small house near the port and even called my grandmother to tell her I was safe.
The next morning the storm had passed, and the sea was as calm as a mirror.
When the ferry finally arrived at the island, my grandmother was waiting at the pier with tears in her eyes.
She later told me that the fisherman's family and hers had helped each other for three generations.
That trip taught me that kindness from strangers can turn a frightening experience into a warm memory.
