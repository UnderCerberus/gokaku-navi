# 第 29 群（段落 2 本・30 文）と修正の変種（第 524 組）
Many students stay up late before an exam, believing that more study time will lead to better results.
However, research suggests that this strategy may actually do more harm than good.
While we sleep, the brain is far from inactive.
It sorts through the information we took in during the day and decides what to keep.
Memories that are judged important are strengthened, while others are allowed to fade.
In one well-known experiment, two groups of students were asked to memorize a list of words.
One group was allowed to sleep afterward, but the other group had to stay awake all night.
When both groups were tested the next day, those who had slept remembered far more words.
Scientists believe this is because sleep gives the brain a chance to move new memories into long-term storage.
Lack of sleep also makes it harder to concentrate, so students who sleep too little may not learn efficiently in class.
This does not mean that studying the night before a test is useless.
Reviewing the material briefly and then getting a good night's sleep is likely to be more effective than studying until dawn.
Of course, the amount of sleep people need varies from person to person.
Most teenagers, though, need around eight to ten hours a night, which is more than many of them actually get.
Getting enough sleep, then, should be seen not as a waste of time but as part of studying.
During the pandemic, many schools had no choice but to move their classes online.
At first, both teachers and students struggled with the new way of learning.
Some students did not have a computer at home, and others had trouble connecting to the internet.
Teachers, for their part, had to learn how to keep their students' attention through a screen.
Even so, online classes turned out to have some unexpected advantages.
Students who lived far from school no longer had to spend hours commuting.
Shy students, who rarely spoke up in the classroom, found it easier to ask questions in the chat.
Recorded lessons also allowed students to review difficult parts as many times as they wanted.
On the other hand, many students said that they missed seeing their friends every day.
Without face-to-face contact, it was hard for them to stay motivated.
In my opinion, the best approach is to combine the strengths of both styles.
Schools could, for example, hold most classes in person while offering recorded lessons for review.
Such a system would allow students to learn at their own pace without losing the benefits of school life.
Technology itself is neither good nor bad; what matters is how we use it.
If we use it wisely, it can make education more flexible and open to everyone.
