Almost everyone has put off an important task at least once, even when they knew it had to be done.
For a long time, people thought that procrastination was simply a sign of laziness.
Recent research, however, suggests that it has more to do with emotions than with time management.
When a task makes us feel anxious or bored, our brains look for a quick way to feel better.
Checking our phones or cleaning our rooms gives us a small sense of relief, so we keep doing it.
The problem is that the relief does not last long, and the task is still waiting for us.
As the deadline gets closer, we feel even worse than we did at the beginning.
Some people believe they work better under pressure, but studies show that the quality of their work usually suffers.
So what can we do to stop putting things off?
One useful method is to break a large task into smaller steps that seem easier to start.
Instead of telling yourself to write an entire report, you might decide to write just the first paragraph.
Once you have started, it is often easier to keep going than you expected.
Another method is to be kind to yourself when you fail.
People who forgive themselves for procrastinating in the past are less likely to do it again.
In short, understanding why we delay can help us take the first step.
When I was in junior high school, my grandmother lived with us for about a year.
She had been a cook at a small restaurant, and she knew how to make almost anything.
At first, I was not interested in cooking at all.
I thought it was something that took too much time and that I could always buy food at a convenience store.
One Sunday, however, she asked me to help her make dumplings for a family party.
I agreed only because I had nothing else to do that afternoon.
She showed me how to fold each dumpling so that it would not open while it was cooking.
My first few attempts looked so strange that we both started laughing.
By the end of the afternoon, though, I had made more than fifty of them by myself.
When my relatives ate them at the party, several of them asked who had made them.
I felt proud, and for the first time I understood why my grandmother enjoyed cooking for others.
After she moved back to her hometown, I kept practicing the recipes she had taught me.
Now I cook dinner for my family at least twice a week.
Whenever I make dumplings, I remember the sound of her laughter in our small kitchen.
Cooking, for me, has become a way to stay connected with someone I love.
