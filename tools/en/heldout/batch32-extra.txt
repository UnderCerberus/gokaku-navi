Every autumn, millions of birds leave their summer homes and fly thousands of kilometers to warmer regions.
Some small birds, which weigh less than a coin, cross entire oceans without stopping to rest.
How they manage to find their way has puzzled scientists for centuries.
One theory is that birds use the position of the sun during the day and the stars at night.
In an experiment in the 1960s, young birds kept in a room with an artificial night sky turned in the direction their species normally flies.
When the researchers rotated the pattern of stars, the birds changed direction as well.
Other studies suggest that birds can sense the earth's magnetic field, almost as if they had a compass inside their heads.
Smell may also play a role, especially for seabirds that travel over open water where there are few landmarks.
Most likely, birds rely on several of these methods at once rather than on just one.
Migration is extremely dangerous, and many birds never complete the journey.
Storms, lack of food, and tall buildings that reflect the sky all take their toll.
Climate change is adding new problems, because the timing of migration no longer matches the times when food is most plentiful.
If birds arrive too late, the insects they depend on may have already disappeared.
Protecting the places where birds stop to rest and feed is therefore essential.
By doing so, we can help make sure that these remarkable journeys continue for generations to come.
