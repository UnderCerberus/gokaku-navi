# 未見文 第 41 群（第 536 組）: 入試長文に近い自作の段落 2 本（垂直農場の説明文 15 文・祖父のフィルムカメラの物語 15 文）を 1 文ずつ
In many large cities, finding enough land to grow food has become increasingly difficult.
One solution that has attracted attention in recent years is the vertical farm, a building in which crops are grown on shelves stacked on top of one another.
Instead of soil, the plants are usually grown in water that contains all the nutrients they need.
Because the buildings are closed, farmers can control the temperature, light, and humidity throughout the year.
As a result, vegetables such as lettuce can be harvested as often as twenty times a year, compared with only a few times on a traditional farm.
Vertical farms also use far less water than ordinary fields, since most of it is collected and reused.
Moreover, because they are built close to the people who buy their products, the food does not have to be transported long distances.
This means that it reaches stores while it is still fresh and that less fuel is burned along the way.
However, vertical farming is not without its problems.
The biggest one is the cost of the electricity needed to run the lights day and night.
Some companies have gone out of business because they could not sell their vegetables at prices low enough to compete with those grown outdoors.
In addition, only a limited number of crops, mostly leafy greens and herbs, can be grown profitably in this way.
Rice and wheat, which feed most of the world's population, require too much space to be grown indoors.
Even so, many experts believe that the technology will become cheaper as it improves.
Vertical farms may never replace traditional agriculture, but they could play an important role in feeding the cities of the future.
When my grandfather passed away last spring, he left me an old film camera that he had used for more than forty years.
At first, I did not know what to do with it, because I had only ever taken pictures with my smartphone.
One weekend, I found a shop in town that still sold film and asked the owner how to load it.
She showed me patiently, and warned me that I could take only thirty-six pictures with one roll.
That made me think carefully before pressing the button, which I had never done with my phone.
I spent the whole afternoon walking around my neighborhood, looking for things worth photographing.
A few days later, I went back to the shop to pick up the prints.
To my disappointment, more than half of them were too dark or out of focus.
Still, there were a few that I really liked, including one of an old woman feeding pigeons in the park.
When I showed the photos to my mother, she suddenly became quiet.
Then she took out a box of pictures that my grandfather had taken when she was a child.
Many of them had been taken in the same park, from almost the same spot.
It was as if my grandfather and I had been looking at the world through the same eyes.
Since then, I have carried the camera with me almost every day.
Each picture I take now feels less like a record of a moment and more like a conversation with someone I miss.
