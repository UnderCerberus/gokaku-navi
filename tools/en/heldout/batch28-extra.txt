# 第 28 群（段落 2 本・30 文）と修正の変種（第 523 組）
Every year, about one third of all the food produced in the world is lost or thrown away.
This means that while millions of people go hungry, a huge amount of food ends up in the garbage.
Food waste happens at every stage, from the farm to the kitchen table.
On farms, vegetables that do not look perfect are often left in the fields because stores refuse to buy them.
In supermarkets, food is removed from the shelves as soon as it reaches its best-before date, even though it is still safe to eat.
At home, many people buy more than they need and forget about the food in the back of their refrigerators.
Throwing away food is not only a waste of money but also harmful to the environment.
When food rots in landfills, it produces methane, a gas that contributes to global warming.
Moreover, the water and energy used to grow and transport the food are wasted as well.
Some countries have started to take action against this problem.
In France, for example, large supermarkets are required by law to donate unsold food to charities.
In Japan, some restaurants now let customers take their leftovers home in special boxes.
Individuals can also make a difference by planning their meals and buying only what they will actually eat.
Small changes in our daily habits, if many people make them, can have a big impact.
After all, the food we save today may help feed someone tomorrow.
When I first arrived in Canada as an exchange student, I could hardly understand what people were saying.
My host family spoke so fast that I often had to ask them to repeat themselves.
At school, I was too shy to raise my hand in class, even when I knew the answer.
One day, my teacher asked me to give a short presentation about Japanese culture.
I was nervous, but I decided to talk about the tea ceremony, which my grandmother had taught me.
To my surprise, my classmates listened carefully and asked me many questions afterward.
Some of them even said that they wanted to visit Japan someday.
That experience gave me the confidence I needed to speak up more often.
From then on, I tried to talk to as many people as possible, even if I made mistakes.
Little by little, my English improved, and I began to enjoy my life there.
Looking back, I think the most important thing I learned was not English itself.
It was the courage to share who I am with people from different backgrounds.
I am now studying hard so that I can work in a job that connects Japan with the rest of the world.
If I had not gone abroad, I might never have discovered what I really want to do.
I would encourage anyone who has the chance to study abroad to take it.
