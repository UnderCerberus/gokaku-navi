# 第 33 群（段落 2 本・30 文）と修正の変種（第 528 組）
Before the fifteenth century, books in Europe had to be copied by hand, which made them rare and expensive.
A single Bible could take a skilled writer more than a year to complete.
Around 1450, a German craftsman named Johannes Gutenberg developed a printing press that used movable metal type.
Each letter was cast as a separate piece, so the same letters could be arranged again and again to print different pages.
With this machine, a small team of workers could produce hundreds of pages in a single day.
The new technology spread quickly, and within fifty years printing shops had appeared in more than two hundred cities.
As books became cheaper, more people had a reason to learn how to read.
Ideas that had once been shared only among a small group of scholars could now reach ordinary people.
Some historians believe that the printing press helped make the scientific revolution possible.
Scientists were able to compare their results with those of researchers in other countries far more easily than before.
Not everyone welcomed these changes, however.
Some authorities worried that the spread of printed material would make it harder for them to control what people thought.
In several countries, books that criticized those in power were banned or burned.
Even so, it proved impossible to stop the flow of information once it had begun.
In many ways, the arguments about the internet today echo the debates that followed Gutenberg's invention.
Many schools today are debating whether students should be allowed to use smartphones during class.
Supporters point out that phones give students instant access to dictionaries, maps, and up-to-date information.
A student who does not understand a word, for example, can look it up in seconds instead of waiting for the teacher.
Phones can also be useful for recording experiments or taking photos of what is written on the board.
On the other hand, critics argue that phones are more likely to distract students than to help them.
It only takes a single message from a friend to break a student's concentration.
Some research even suggests that the mere presence of a phone on the desk lowers performance on tests.
In my opinion, the answer is neither to ban phones completely nor to allow them without limits.
Instead, teachers should decide when phones may be used, depending on the purpose of each lesson.
During a test or a discussion, for instance, phones could be kept in bags.
When students are doing research, on the other hand, they could be allowed to take them out.
Clear rules like these would help students learn to use technology responsibly.
After all, they will have to manage their own phone use once they start working.
Learning that skill at school may be just as important as learning the content of any subject.
For these reasons, I believe a flexible approach is better than a strict ban.
