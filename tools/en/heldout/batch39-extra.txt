# 第 39 群（段落 2 本・30 文）と修正の変種（第 534 組）
For a long time, many scientists believed that only humans could experience complex emotions such as joy, grief, or jealousy.
Animals, they argued, simply reacted to their surroundings according to instinct.
In recent decades, however, a growing number of studies have challenged this view.
Elephants, for example, have been observed staying beside the body of a dead family member for hours, sometimes touching it gently with their trunks.
Some researchers think this behavior may be a sign of mourning, although others are more cautious.
Dogs, too, seem to show emotions that resemble our own.
In one experiment, dogs that saw their owners pay attention to a toy dog behaved in ways that suggested jealousy.
They pushed the toy away and tried to get between it and their owners.
Even animals that are not usually thought of as intelligent, like fish and bees, may have simple emotional states.
Bees that had been given a sweet reward, for instance, acted more optimistically when they were faced with an unclear situation.
Of course, it is difficult to know exactly what an animal is feeling, because animals cannot tell us in words.
Scientists must rely on careful observation and avoid reading too much into what they see.
Still, the evidence suggests that the line between humans and other animals is not as clear as it once seemed.
This has important implications for how we treat animals on farms, in zoos, and in our homes.
If animals can suffer, then we have a responsibility to reduce their suffering whenever we can.
When I turned sixteen, I got my first part-time job at a small bakery near the station.
The owner, Mr. Kato, had been baking bread there for more than thirty years.
On my first morning, I had to arrive at six o'clock, which was much earlier than I usually got up.
Mr. Kato handed me an apron and told me to watch carefully before touching anything.
I was amazed at how quickly his hands moved as he shaped the dough into dozens of identical rolls.
My job was to put the bread on the shelves and help customers choose what they wanted.
At first, I kept forgetting the names of the different kinds of bread, and I often had to ask Mr. Kato for help.
He never got angry, though; instead, he patiently explained each one again.
After about a month, I could answer most customers' questions without checking the labels.
One rainy afternoon, an elderly woman came in and asked for a loaf that we had already sold out of.
She looked so disappointed that I offered to set one aside for her the next day.
When she came back the following morning, she thanked me with a warm smile and bought two loaves instead of one.
That small experience taught me that good service is about paying attention to people's feelings, not just selling products.
I still work at the bakery on weekends, and Mr. Kato has recently started teaching me how to make bread myself.
Someday I hope to open a bakery of my own where people can enjoy fresh bread and friendly conversation.
