# 未見文 第 42 群（第 537 組）: 入試長文に近い自作の段落 2 本（朝型・夜型と登校時刻の説明文 15 文・体育祭のリレーの物語 15 文）を 1 文ずつ
Some people jump out of bed full of energy, while others can hardly open their eyes before nine o'clock.
Scientists call these natural differences in sleep timing chronotypes.
Your chronotype is controlled partly by your genes and partly by your age.
Teenagers, for example, tend to fall asleep later and wake up later than both young children and adults.
This is not simply because they stay up playing games or chatting with friends.
During adolescence, the body begins to release the sleep hormone melatonin about two hours later than it did in childhood.
Unfortunately, most schools start early in the morning, so many students arrive in class without having had enough sleep.
Studies have shown that students who sleep less than seven hours a night find it harder to concentrate and remember what they have learned.
In some parts of the United States, schools that moved their starting time later saw improvements in both attendance and test scores.
Fewer students fell asleep during lessons, and the number of car accidents involving teenage drivers also went down.
Not everyone agrees that later start times are the answer, however.
Parents who work early may find it difficult to take their children to school, and after-school activities would have to end later in the evening.
Some experts suggest that students should instead be taught how to manage their sleep better.
Turning off screens an hour before bed, for instance, can make it much easier to fall asleep.
Whatever the solution, it is clear that sleep deserves more attention than it usually receives.
In my second year of junior high school, I was chosen to run the last leg of the class relay at the sports festival.
I was not the fastest runner in our class, but our teacher said that she trusted me to stay calm under pressure.
For weeks, the four of us practiced passing the baton every day after school until it was almost dark.
On the day of the festival, the sky was clear and the playground was filled with families holding cameras.
When the race began, our first runner got off to a great start and handed the baton to the second runner in first place.
By the time it reached me, however, we had fallen to third.
I took the baton and ran as hard as I could, hearing nothing but my own breathing.
Halfway around the track, I passed one runner, and the crowd began to shout.
Just a few meters before the finish line, my foot slipped and I fell to the ground.
For a moment I could not move, and I was sure that I had ruined everything.
Then I heard my classmates calling my name, so I got up and ran the rest of the way.
We finished last, and I could not stop crying as I walked back to our seats.
To my surprise, nobody blamed me; instead, they patted me on the back and told me that I had done my best.
Later, our teacher told us that she was prouder of how we had supported each other than she would have been of any medal.
I still remember that day whenever I feel like giving up.
