# 未見文 第 34 群（第 529 組）: 入試長文に近い自作の段落 2 本（あくびの科学の説明文 15 文・レストランでのアルバイト体験の物語 15 文）を 1 文ずつ
Almost everyone yawns several times a day, yet scientists still do not fully understand why.
For many years, the most popular explanation was that yawning brings more oxygen into the body.
However, experiments in which people breathed air with extra oxygen did not reduce how often they yawned.
A newer theory suggests that yawning helps cool the brain when it becomes too warm.
Supporters of this idea point out that people tend to yawn more in winter than in summer, when the outside air is too hot to cool anything.
Another interesting fact is that yawning seems to be contagious.
Simply seeing, hearing, or even reading about someone else yawning can make you do the same.
You may have noticed this while reading the last two sentences.
Contagious yawning appears to be linked to empathy, the ability to share the feelings of others.
Studies have found that people are more likely to catch a yawn from family members and close friends than from strangers.
Interestingly, children under the age of four rarely yawn in response to others.
This may be because the skills needed to understand other people's feelings are still developing at that age.
Some animals, including chimpanzees and dogs, also seem to catch yawns.
Dogs, for example, have been observed yawning after their owners did.
Although such a common action may seem unimportant, studying it could tell us a great deal about how our brains work.
Last summer, I started working part-time at a small Italian restaurant near my house.
On my first day, the owner, Mr. Rossi, showed me around the kitchen and explained what I would be doing.
My main job was to take orders and carry plates to the tables.
It sounded easy, but I soon realized how much there was to remember.
During the lunch rush, I mixed up two orders and brought the wrong dishes to a family with small children.
The children started crying, and I wanted to disappear.
To my surprise, Mr. Rossi did not get angry at me.
Instead, he apologized to the family himself and offered them free desserts.
After they left, he told me that everyone makes mistakes when they are new.
"What matters is that you learn from them," he said with a smile.
From then on, I started writing every order down carefully before going to the kitchen.
I also began checking each plate twice before carrying it out.
By the end of the summer, I could handle even the busiest hours without much trouble.
More than the skills I gained, though, I will remember the way Mr. Rossi treated me.
If I ever become a manager myself, I hope I can be as patient with others as he was with me.
