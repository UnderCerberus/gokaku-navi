# 未見文 第 37 群（第 532 組）: 入試長文に近い自作の段落 2 本（習慣のしくみの説明文 15 文・スピーチコンテストに出た物語 15 文）を 1 文ずつ
Psychologists estimate that about forty percent of what we do every day is not the result of conscious decisions but of habits.
A habit forms when a behavior is repeated so often in the same situation that the brain begins to perform it automatically.
This saves a great deal of mental energy, which can then be used for more difficult tasks.
However, the same process that helps us brush our teeth without thinking can also make bad habits very hard to break.
According to researchers, every habit consists of three parts: a cue, a routine, and a reward.
The cue is something that triggers the behavior, such as a time of day, a place, or a particular feeling.
For example, a student who feels bored while studying may reach for a smartphone almost without noticing it.
Checking messages gives a small sense of pleasure, and this reward makes the student more likely to repeat the behavior the next time.
Rather than trying to stop a bad habit through willpower alone, experts suggest changing the cue or replacing the routine with a better one.
Someone who wants to stop eating snacks at night, for instance, might brush their teeth right after dinner.
It is also easier to build a new habit if you start with something so small that it seems almost too easy.
A person who hopes to exercise regularly could begin by doing just five push-ups every morning.
Once the action becomes automatic, it can gradually be made longer or more difficult.
It usually takes more than two months for a new behavior to become a habit, so patience is important.
In the end, the small things we do every day shape who we become far more than the big decisions we make once in a while.
When my English teacher asked me to take part in the city speech contest, my first reaction was to say no.
I had never spoken in front of a large audience before, and the thought of forgetting my words terrified me.
My teacher, however, told me that the purpose of the contest was not to win but to share my ideas with others.
I finally agreed and decided to talk about my grandmother, who had moved to Japan from Brazil fifty years earlier.
For the next three weeks, I practiced my speech every evening in front of the mirror until I could say it without looking at my notes.
My little brother listened to it so many times that he could recite the first part himself.
On the day of the contest, my hands were shaking as I waited for my turn behind the stage.
The student who spoke just before me gave an excellent speech about climate change, and the audience applauded loudly.
When I walked to the microphone, I saw my grandmother sitting in the third row with a big smile on her face.
Seeing her there made me feel calmer, and I began to speak more slowly than I had planned.
Halfway through the speech, I forgot one sentence completely, but instead of stopping, I simply went on to the next part.
To my surprise, nobody seemed to notice the mistake.
I did not win a prize, but after the contest several people came up to me and said that my story had moved them.
My grandmother hugged me and told me that she had never been so proud in her life.
Since that day, I have been much less afraid of speaking in public, and I am even thinking about entering the contest again next year.
